Introduction to Macroeconomics

What economics is · micro vs macro · goals & policies · types of variables · key indicators · schools of thought.

1.1 What economics is

Economics = the science of scarcity and choice

Economics studies how individuals and societies choose to use the scarce resources that nature and previous generations provided. Because resources are limited but wants are unlimited, choice is unavoidable.

Scarcity — we "cannot have it all", so we must decide what to have and what to forgo.
Trade-off — to get one thing you give up another.
Opportunity cost — the value of the next best alternative you gave up. This is the single most important idea in the whole course.
Intuition“There is no such thing as a free lunch.” Even a “free” hour of Netflix costs you the study (or sleep) you could have done instead — that forgone best option is its true cost.

The three basic questions every society answers

  1. What to produce?
  2. How to produce it?
  3. For whom — who gets the goods and services?

Micro vs. Macro

Microeconomics zooms in — individual industries, firms and households (one market's price & quantity).

Macroeconomics zooms out — the whole nation's aggregates: total income, employment, output, the price level.

Positive vs. Normative

Positive = facts & cause-effect, no value judgment (“what is”). e.g. “A tax cut raised spending 2%.”

Normative = value judgments (“what should be”). e.g. “The government ought to cut taxes.” Policy debates are normative.

1.2 Objectives & policies

The four goals — and the two big policy levers

Economists judge outcomes against four criteria:

Efficiency (allocative) — produce what people want at the least possible cost.
Equity — fairness in how output is shared.
Growth — an increase in the economy's total output over time.
Stability — output grows steadily, with low inflation and full employment.
The two policies (memorise the split) Fiscal policy = the Government's taxing & spending (Chapter 4).
Monetary policy = the Central Bank's tools to control the quantity of money.
1.3 Types of variables

Stock vs. Flow, and Nominal vs. Real

Stock vs. Flow

Stock = measured at an instant (a photograph). Flow = measured over a period (a video).

Test yourself “FDI during 2016–2020” is a flow. “Total FDI held on 31 Dec 2020” is a stock. Wealth = stock; income & saving = flows.

Nominal vs. Real

Nominal = not adjusted for price changes. Real = adjusted for prices (divide out inflation).

Real income = YP  ·  Real interest rate r = i − inflation
Why it mattersOnly real variables tell you about actual purchasing power. A 5% raise with 8% inflation makes you poorer in real terms.
1.4 Key indicators

The dashboard of the economy

Macro health is read from three groups of indicators:

  1. Level & growth of output — GDP and its growth rate.
  2. Internal stability — inflation, unemployment, interest rates, income distribution.
  3. External stability — balance of payments, international reserves, external debt, exchange rate.

① Output & the business cycle

Aggregate output = total goods & services produced in a period. The business cycle is its short-run ups and downs around a rising long-run trend.

Definitions to nail Recession = output falls for two consecutive quarters.   Depression = a prolonged, deep recession.

② Prices & inflation

Inflation — a rise in the overall price level. Deflation — a fall. Hyperinflation — very rapid inflation.
CPI (Consumer Price Index) & PPI (Producer Price Index) measure the price level.
Core inflation strips out volatile items (energy, fresh food) to reveal the underlying trend.
Inflation rateyear t = CPIt − CPIt−1CPIt−1 × 100

③ Unemployment

Population = Labour force + Non-labour force
Labour-force participation rate = Labour forceWorking-age population × 100
Unemployment rate = UnemployedLabour force × 100  ← the blank: divide by the labour force, not population
Three types of unemployment Frictional — normal short-run job search / people between jobs (always exists).
Structural — skills/industries no longer match the economy (e.g. automation).
Cyclical — caused by recessions (demand falls).
Natural rate of unemployment = Frictional + Structural. It's the unemployment that remains even at "full employment" — cyclical unemployment is zero at the natural rate.

④ Interest rates

Real interest rate = Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate

Creditors (lenders/savers) want the real rate high; debtors (borrowers) want it low. Loan rate > deposit rate — the spread is the bank's margin.

⑤ Income distribution — Lorenz curve & Gini

Gini coefficient = Area AArea A + Area B
Read the number Gini ranges 0 → 1. 0 = perfect equality (everyone the same), 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Higher Gini = more unequal income distribution.
1.5 History of macroeconomic thought

From Classical to Keynesian — the big argument that runs through this course

Classical (Adam Smith, 1776) — the market's “invisible hand” coordinates self-interest into social good; division of labour raises output. Reaction against mercantilism.
Marx — focused on the unfair distribution of industrial gains; history as “class struggle” between owners of capital and labour.
Neo-classical (Marshall) — “father of modern economics”; markets are efficient & self-regulating; introduced homo economicus (rational self-interested agent).
Keynes (1936, Great Depression) — markets do not always self-correct; in slumps the government should boost aggregate demand (spend, cut interest rates, invest).
Monetarists (Friedman) — crises come from bad monetary policy; government should control the money supply and leave goods markets alone. Led to neo-liberalism (privatise, free trade).
The distinction you must remember all term Classical = the long run: prices & wages are flexible, economy sits at full employment.
Keynesian = the short run: prices & wages are sticky, economy can sit below full employment — so policy has room to work.
Keynes: “In the long run we are all dead.”

National Income & Product Accounts (GDP)

Circular flow · what GDP is · GDP vs GNP · the three ways to measure it · nominal vs real & the deflator · limitations.

2.1–2.2 The players & the circular flow

Five sectors, one loop of money

Macro tracks five groups: (1) Households + (2) Firms = the private sector, (3) Government, (4) Rest of the world, and (5) Financial institutions.

The circular flow shows income and payments moving between them. Two forces act on the loop:

Injections (money in) Investment I, Government spending G, Exports X.
Withdrawals / Leakages (money out) Saving S, Taxes T, Imports M.
2.3 What GDP is

Gross Domestic Product — read the definition word-by-word

GDP = total market value of all final goods & services produced within a period by factors of production located within a country.
Final goods = for final use. Intermediate goods = used up in producing something else — not counted directly (avoids double counting).
Value added = value of output leaving a stage − cost of inputs entering it. Summing value added = summing final value.
Located within the country — GDP is about where production happens, not who owns the factors.
What GDP excludes Used goods & pure paper transactions (a house built 3 years ago is not in this year's GDP). But the agent's commission on selling it is — that's a new service produced this year. Only new, current production counts.
2.4 GDP vs GNP

Where vs. Whose

GDP — output produced inside the country (any owner).

GNP — output produced by a country's citizens/factors (anywhere in the world).

GNP = GDP + income of Thai factors used abroad − income of foreign factors used in Thailand
The correction term is called Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIEA).
ExampleProfit of a Thai-owned factory in Laos → adds to Thai GNP (not Thai GDP). A Japanese-owned factory in Bangkok → adds to Thai GDP (not Thai GNP).
2.5 Three ways to measure GDP

Same number, three doors — they must all agree

(a) Production (value-added) approach

Sum the value added at every stage. Worked from the slide (orange-juice example):

StageSales valueValue added
Oranges (farm)500500
Orange juice650150
Juice bottled (factory)900250
Retail sale (supermarket)1,200300
Total value added—1,200
The pointTotal value added (1,200) = the final retail value (1,200). That's why we don't add up all the sales (500+650+900+1200) — that would double-count the oranges four times.

(b) Expenditure approach — the one you'll use most

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)
C — Consumption: household spending. Durable goods, non-durables, and services.
I — Gross private investment: new capital by firms/households = non-residential (machines/plant) + residential (new housing) + change in inventories.
G — Government consumption & investment (excludes transfer payments like welfare — no new output).
X − M — Net exports: exports minus imports (can be negative).
Inventories — filled-in example Begin: 2 units. Produce: 7. Sell: 6. End: 3 units (×10฿).
Change in inventories = end − beginning = 3 − 2 = 1 unit = 10 ฿. This counts as investment even though it wasn't sold — it was produced this year.
GDP = final sales + change in inventories + other components.
Gross vs Net Net investment = Gross investment − Depreciation.
NDP = GDP − Depreciation.   So NDP = C + (I − Depreciation) + G + (X − M).

(c) Income approach

National income = Factor incomes + (indirect taxes − subsidies) + net business transfers + surplus of gov. enterprises
Factor incomes = compensation of employees + proprietors' income + corporate profits + net interest + rental income.
The full ladder (very exam-friendly) GDP → + NFIEA → GNP → − Depreciation → NNP → − Statistical discrepancy → NI → − (retained earnings + other non-household parts) → PI → − personal income tax → DI.
And disposable income splits: DI = C + S (if no personal interest/transfer payments).
2.6 Nominal vs Real GDP & the deflator

Separating "more stuff" from "higher prices"

Nominal GDP = Σ (Pcurrent × Qcurrent)   — uses this year's prices
Real GDP    = Σ (Pbase year × Qcurrent)   — holds prices fixed at the base year
GDP deflator = Nominal GDPReal GDP × 100
Why we bother Nominal GDP can rise just because prices rose. Real GDP strips prices out, so it measures genuine growth in output. The deflator is the economy-wide price level implied by that comparison. In the base year, Real = Nominal, so the deflator = 100.

→ Full numeric solution (base year = Year 3) is in Worked Example A2.

2.7 Limitations of GDP

GDP is not the same as well-being

GDP & welfareIgnores crime, leisure, pollution and how income is distributed (use GDP per capita + Gini for that).
GDP & measurementMisses the underground economy and non-market work (housework, volunteering) — real production that never shows up.

National Income & Equilibrium Determination

The Keynesian core: consumption & saving · investment · the DAE line · equilibrium (two methods) · the multiplier · paradox of thrift · GDP gaps.

3.1 The key idea

Desired vs. Actual — the engine of Keynesian economics

Keynes built the model on desired (planned) spending, not actual spending. The gap between them is unplanned inventory, and it's what pushes the economy toward equilibrium.

Actual Aggregate Expenditure = Actual C + Actual I  (always = output, by accounting)
DAE (Desired Aggregate Expenditure) = Desired C + Desired I + G + (X − M)
The crucial split — investment Actual I = Planned I + Unplanned inventory investment. When firms can't sell what they made, goods pile up as unplanned inventory (actual > planned). When they sell more than expected, inventories fall below plan (actual < planned). This mismatch is the signal to change production.
3.2.1 Consumption & saving

The consumption function C = Ca + bYd

Keynes: the main driver of consumption is disposable income Yd = Y − T. Using the slide's numbers, C = 100 + 0.6Yd.

Ca = autonomous consumption (spending even at zero income = 100)
b = MPC = ΔCΔYd = slope = 0.6   → of each extra ฿, 60 satang is spent
APC = CYd (average)    MPC = marginal (the slope)
Golden rule MPC + MPS = 1. Every extra baht is either spent or saved. Here MPC = 0.6, so MPS = 0.4.

Saving is just the mirror image

S = Yd − C = Yd − (Ca + bYd) = −Ca + (1 − b)Yd
S = −100 + 0.4Yd   → intercept −Ca, slope = MPS = 0.4
Break-even income (where C = Yd, S = 0) 100 + 0.6Yd = Yd → 0.4Yd = 100 → Yd* = 250. Below it households dissave (borrow / sell assets); above it they save.
Move ALONG vs. SHIFT (classic exam question) A change in Yd → move along the C (or S) curve. A change in any other factor (interest rate, expectations, wealth, population) → the whole curve shifts.
Two rival theories (know the one-liners) Permanent Income (Friedman): consumption depends on permanent (expected long-run) income, not this year's. A one-off bonus is mostly saved.
Life-Cycle (Ando & Modigliani): people smooth consumption over a lifetime — borrow when young, save in middle age, dissave in retirement.
3.2.2–3.2.4 The other components

Investment, Government & Net exports

Investment I

Driven by the real interest rate (↑r → ↓I) and income (↑Y → ↑I), plus technology, existing capital, taxes, expectations.

Autonomous: I = Ia
Induced: I = Ia + dY
Invest when… NPV > 0, or equivalently MEC (IRR) > i — the project's return beats the market interest rate.

Accelerator principle: investment demand is derived from the change in output — a rising ΔY accelerates net investment.

Government G & Net exports (X−M)

G is set by policy, planned in advance → treated as autonomous (G = Ga, flat line vs. Y). Expansionary policy shifts it up; contractionary shifts it down.

Exports X depend on foreign demand → autonomous (X = Xa).

Imports rise with income: M = Ma + mY
m = MPM = ΔMΔY
3.2 The DAE line

Add it all up

DAE = C + I + G + (X − M)
= (Ca − bTa + Ia + Ga + Xa − Ma) + (b + d − m)Y
↑ autonomous part (the intercept)      ↑ slope of the DAE line
Slope of DAE = MPC + MPI − MPM = (b + d − m). Every extra ฿ of income induces this much extra spending. It's always < 1, which is what makes equilibrium stable and the multiplier finite.
3.3 Equilibrium — Method 1

Y = DAE (the "Keynesian cross")

Equilibrium = no tendency to change. In the goods market that means output equals desired spending:

Y = DAE
Equilibrium formulas (memorise the pattern) Closed, no gov:   YE = 11 − b(Ca + Ia)
Closed, with gov: YE = 11 − b(Ca − bTa + Ia + Ga)
Open, with gov:    YE = 11 − b + m(Ca − bTa + Ia + Ga + Xa − Ma)
Worked (closed, with gov) C=100+0.6Yd, I=40, T=10, G=60 → YE = 2.5 [100 − 0.6(10) + 40 + 60] = 2.5(194) = 485.
3.3 Equilibrium — Method 2

Injection = Leakage (must give the same YE)

At equilibrium the money added to the loop equals the money drained from it:

Closed, no gov:   I = S
Closed, with gov: I + G = S + T
Open, with gov:   I + G + X = S + T + M

→ Both methods solved on the same numbers in A3 & A4 — and they agree (YE = 880).

3.3 The multiplier

Why a small push moves income a lot

Spend 1,000฿ building a factory. The builders earn 1,000฿; they spend MPC of it (600฿); those receivers spend 600×0.6 = 360฿; and so on — an infinite but shrinking chain:

ΔY = 1000(1 + 0.6 + 0.6² + …) = 1000 × 11 − 0.6 = 1000 × 2.5 = 2,500
Multiplier formulas Simple spending multiplier k = 11 − MPC = 1MPS.
More leakages → smaller multiplier. Adding an income tax and imports changes the denominator to (1 − slope of DAE). Higher MPC (or lower MPS) → bigger multiplier.
Tax & balanced-budget multipliers (closed economy) Government-spending multiplier = 11−b.   Tax multiplier = −b1−b (smaller & negative — a tax cut is weaker than equal spending, because part is saved).
Balanced-budget multiplier = 1: raise G and T by the same amount and Y rises by exactly that amount.
3.4 Paradox of thrift

If everyone saves more, everyone earns less

An individual saving more is prudent. But if everyone tries to save more at once, aggregate demand falls → firms cut output → income falls → and society may end up saving the same (or less). This is a fallacy of composition: what's true for one isn't true for all.

Policy takeawayShort run (fight a slump): encourage spending (↓S, ↑C). Long run (fund growth): encourage saving (↑S) so there are funds for investment.
3.5 GDP gaps

Inflationary vs. Deflationary gap

Compare equilibrium output YE with full-employment (potential) output YF.

The gap is the vertical distance between the DAE line and the 45° line, measured right at YF.

3.6 Keynes vs Classical vs in-between (the multiplier's size depends on prices) Keynesian: below full employment, prices sticky → when DAE rises, only Y rises → biggest multiplier.
Classical: at full employment, prices flexible → DAE↑ mostly raises P, not Y → smallest multiplier.
Non-Keynes non-classic: below full employment but prices do move → in between.
So ΔY: Keynes > in-between > Classical.

Fiscal Policy

Government spending & taxing · tools · tax structures · public debt · budget types · automatic vs discretionary · policy problems.

4.1–4.3 Meaning & tools

Fiscal policy = the government's spending & taxing choices

Its objectives mirror the four macro goals: efficient allocation, fair distribution, growth, and stability. Four tools:

Government expenditure (G) — consumption, investment, and loan/interest payments.
Transfer payments (R) — welfare, unemployment benefits, pensions, veterans' benefits. Not counted in G/GDP — no new output, just redistribution.
Revenue (T) — mostly taxes (below), plus non-tax revenue (state enterprises, fees, fines).
Public debt — borrowing to finance a deficit.
Direct vs Indirect tax Direct — the payer cannot shift the burden (personal & corporate income tax, inheritance, property tax).
Indirect — the payer can shift it onto others (VAT/sales tax, excise, customs duties).

4.3.4 Public debt & budgets

Borrowing and the budget balance

Public debt is classified by term (short < 1 yr, long > 5 yr) and source (domestic vs. international). It affects price stability, resource allocation, income distribution, and the state's ability to run projects.

Three budget types (G + R vs T) Deficit: G + R > T (spend more than you take) · Surplus: G + R < T · Balanced: G + R = T.
In accounting terms total spending is financed by revenue + public debt + treasury reserves.
4.4 Types of fiscal policy

Automatic vs. discretionary — and expansion vs. contraction

Non-discretionary = Automatic stabilisers Work by themselves, no new law needed. Boom → tax take rises & welfare falls automatically → cools the economy. Slump → taxes fall & welfare rises automatically → cushions the fall.
Tools: income tax (T = Ta + tY) and transfer payments (R = Ra − gY).
Discretionary Deliberate changes to tax types, tax rates, or G when automatic stabilisers aren't enough. Slump → ↑G / ↓T (expansionary). Overheating → ↓G / ↑T (contractionary).
Match the disease to the cure Recession / deflationary gap → Expansionary: ↑G, ↓T → DAE↑ → Y↑.
Inflation / inflationary gap → Contractionary: ↓G, ↑T → DAE↓ → Y↓.
4.4 Why fiscal policy is hard

Six real-world problems

  1. Lags — recognition → decision → execution → response. By the time it acts, the problem may have changed.
  2. Irreversibility — programmes are hard to switch off once the crisis passes.
  3. Expectations — if people think a stimulus is temporary, they don't change behaviour.
  4. Political goals can conflict with economic stability (spending before elections).
  5. Extra saving — an expansion fails to stimulate if households just save the extra income (paradox of thrift again).
  6. Crowding-out — government borrowing pushes up interest rates and displaces private investment.
Thailand's Sustainable Fiscal Framework (targets) Public debt / GDP < 60% ✓ · Debt / budget < 15% ✓ · Balanced budget ✗ (government chose to stimulate) · Investment share of spending > 25% ✗.

Assignment 3 · Equilibrium (Y = DAE)

Open economy with government — every step

Given: C = 80 + 0.4Yd, I = 50 + 0.6Y, G = 30, T = 10 + 0.1Y, X = 40, M = 20 + 0.16Y, with Yd = Y − T.

Step 1 — substitute to get C in terms of Y

Yd = Y − T = Y − (10 + 0.1Y) = −10 + 0.9Y
C = 80 + 0.4(−10 + 0.9Y) = 80 − 4 + 0.36Y = 76 + 0.36Y

Step 2 — build DAE

DAE = C + I + G + X − M
= (76 + 0.36Y) + (50 + 0.6Y) + 30 + 40 − (20 + 0.16Y)
= (76+50+30+40−20) + (0.36+0.6−0.16)Y = 176 + 0.80Y

Step 3 — set Y = DAE (Question 1)

Y = 176 + 0.8Y → 0.2Y = 176 → YE = 880

Question 3 — multipliers (the important, subtle part)

The DAE slope is k = b(1−t) + d − m = 0.4(0.9) + 0.6 − 0.16 = 0.80, so the common multiplier is:

11 − k = 11 − 0.8 = 5
Autonomous variableMultiplier (∂Y/∂·)
Ca, Ia, Ga, Xa (spending)+5
Ma (imports — a leakage)−5
Ta (autonomous tax) = −b/(1−k)−0.4/0.2 = −2

Meaning: +1 unit of autonomous G raises equilibrium income by 5 units; +1 unit of autonomous tax lowers it by 2 units.

Careful — a genuine inconsistency in the lecture slides Some slides compute these multipliers with the shortcut 1/(1−b+m) = 1/0.76 ≈ 1.32. That shortcut is only valid when investment and taxes are purely autonomous. Here investment has an induced part (+0.6Y) and tax is induced (+0.1Y), which is exactly why YE came out as 880 (using slope 0.8). To stay consistent with that same YE, the correct multiplier is 1/(1−0.8) = 5, and the tax multiplier is −2. Use the reduced-form (slope) method and you can't go wrong.

Question 4 — ΔGa = +4 and ΔTa = +20

ΔY = (G-multiplier)ΔGa + (T-multiplier)ΔTa = (+5)(4) + (−2)(20) = 20 − 40 = −20
New YE = 880 − 20 = 860

Check by re-deriving: new G=34, T=30+0.1Y → C=68+0.36Y → DAE=172+0.8Y → YE=172/0.2=860 ✓

Question 5 — if current income is 700 (below YE)

At Y = 700, DAE = 176 + 0.8(700) = 736 > 700. Desired spending exceeds output → inventories fall unexpectedly → firms raise production → income rises. The process continues until Y = 880, the equilibrium.

Assignment 4 · Equilibrium (Injection = Leakage)

Same economy, second method — should give YE = 880 again

Step 1 — saving function

S = Yd − C = Yd − (80 + 0.4Yd) = −80 + 0.6Yd
with Yd = −10 + 0.9Y → S = −80 + 0.6(−10 + 0.9Y) = −86 + 0.54Y

Step 2 — set Injection = Leakage (Question 1)

Injection: I + G + X = (50 + 0.6Y) + 30 + 40 = 120 + 0.6Y
Leakage: S + T + M = (−86 + 0.54Y) + (10 + 0.1Y) + (20 + 0.16Y) = −56 + 0.8Y
120 + 0.6Y = −56 + 0.8Y → 176 = 0.2Y → YE = 880 ✓ (matches Method 1)

Question 4 — autonomous saving rises 7 units (Paradox of Thrift)

More autonomous saving = less autonomous consumption (Ca effectively ↓7). Saving is a leakage, so with multiplier 5:

ΔY = −5 × 7 = −35 → New YE = 880 − 35 = 845
LessonTrying to save more made national income fall — the paradox of thrift in one calculation.

Question 5 — if current income is 950 (above YE)

At Y = 950, leakage (−56 + 0.8·950 = 704) > injection (120 + 0.6·950 = 690). Output exceeds desired spending → goods pile up as unplanned inventory → firms cut production → income falls until Y = 880.

1. In the goods market, equilibrium national income occurs where…

Equilibrium = no tendency to change: what's produced (Y) exactly matches what people want to spend (DAE). Equivalently, injections = leakages.

2. If MPC = 0.8 (closed economy, no tax), the simple spending multiplier is…

k = 1/(1 − MPC) = 1/(1 − 0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5. Higher MPC ⟹ bigger multiplier.

3. An increase in autonomous taxes will make equilibrium income…

Taxes are a leakage → they reduce disposable income and spending. The tax multiplier is negative (−b/(1−b)), so ↑T lowers Y.

4. Which is counted in this year's GDP?

GDP counts new, final production this year. Used goods and pure financial transactions aren't new output; flour is an intermediate good (counted inside the bread's value).

5. When current output is below equilibrium (DAE > Y), what pushes the economy back up?

Desired spending exceeds output → firms sell more than they made → unplanned inventory fall → they produce more → Y rises to YE.

6. The paradox of thrift says that if everyone tries to save more at once…

More saving = less spending = lower DAE → output and income fall. A fallacy of composition: good for one person, harmful if everyone does it at once.

7. Real GDP differs from nominal GDP because real GDP…

Real GDP holds prices fixed at a base year, so changes reflect genuine output growth — not inflation. Nominal uses current prices.

8. To close an inflationary gap, contractionary fiscal policy would…

An inflationary gap = too much demand. Reduce DAE by ↓G or ↑T to bring Y back to full-employment output.

Limits & Continuity

What a limit is · one/two-sided · limit laws · 0/0 tricks · trig limits · limits at infinity & asymptotes · continuity.

1.1 The idea

A limit is where a function is heading — not where it lands

Definition. limx→a f(x) = L means we can make f(x) as close to L as we like by taking x close enough to a (from both sides) — without ever letting x = a.
The golden existence rule The two-sided limit exists only if both one-sided limits exist AND are equal:
limx→a f(x) = L  ⟺  limx→a⁻ f(x) = limx→a⁺ f(x) = L
If the left and right sides disagree, the limit does not exist (DNE). The value f(a) itself is irrelevant to the limit.
1.2 Computing limits

Step 1: just plug in. Step 2: if you get 0/0, fix it.

Direct substitution works for polynomials, sin, cos, eˣ, and (on their domains) tan, ln, roots, etc. — just compute f(a).

The three outcomes of a fraction lim f/g ① g(a) ≠ 0 → answer is just f(a)/g(a).
② f(a) ≠ 0, g(a) = 0 → limit is infinite → DNE (a vertical asymptote).
③ f(a) = 0, g(a) = 0 → indeterminate 0/0 — you must rearrange (factor or rationalize) before the limit means anything.

0/0 trick A — factor & cancel

Ex. limx→2 (x²−4)/(x−2). Sub gives 0/0. Factor: (x−2)(x+2)/(x−2) = x+2 → limit = 4.

0/0 trick B — rationalize (multiply by the conjugate)

Ex. limx→0 (√(x²+4) − 2)/x². Multiply top & bottom by (√(x²+4)+2):
= limx→0 (x²+4) − 4x²(√(x²+4)+2) = limx→0 1√(x²+4)+2 = 12+2 = ¼
Handy algebra p²−q² = (p−q)(p+q) · p³±q³ = (p±q)(p²∓pq+q²). These are what let you cancel the troublesome factor.
1.3 Trig limits

Memorise two, derive the rest

limx→0 sin(x)x = 1      limx→0 1 − cos(x)x = 0
Ex. limx→0 sin(2x)/(5x). Force a matching "sin(2x)/(2x)": multiply/divide by 2 → (2/5)·[sin(2x)/(2x)] → (2/5)(1) = 2/5.  General: lim sin(kx)/(mx) = k/m.
Squeeze (sandwich) theorem If f(x) ≤ h(x) ≤ g(x) and both outer limits equal L, then lim h(x) = L too. Classic: since −x² ≤ x²sin(1/x) ≤ x² and both ends → 0, we get limx→0 x²sin(1/x) = 0 (even though sin(1/x) alone has no limit).
1.4 Limits at infinity & asymptotes

End behaviour of a function

Rational functions: compare the top & bottom degrees For limx→±∞ p(x)/q(x), keep only the highest-power term on top and bottom:
  • deg(top) < deg(bottom) → limit = 0 (horizontal asymptote y = 0).
  • deg(top) = deg(bottom) → limit = ratio of leading coefficients (that's the H.A.).
  • deg(top) > deg(bottom) → limit = ±∞ (no horizontal asymptote).
Ex. limx→∞ (1+2x²−3x³)/(2x³+3x) → keep −3x³/2x³ = −3/2 (equal degrees).
"∞ − ∞" → rationalize limx→∞ (√(x²+5x) − x) ×conjugate → 5x/(√(x²+5x)+x) → 5/2 = 5/2. (Use √(x²)=|x|=x since x→∞.)
1.5 Continuity

Continuous = "you can draw it without lifting your pen"

f is continuous at x = a if ALL three hold ① f(a) is defined  ·  ② limx→a f(x) exists  ·  ③ they're equal: limx→a f(x) = f(a).

Exam-style: find the constants that make f continuous

Let f(x) = { (x−2)²/(x²−4) + 2k, x>2 ;   h, x=2 ;   2x+k, x<2 }. Find h, k so f is continuous at x=2.
Left limit (x→2⁻): 2x+k → 4+k.
Right limit (x→2⁺): simplify (x−2)²/[(x−2)(x+2)] = (x−2)/(x+2) → 0, so → 0 + 2k = 2k.
Value: f(2) = h.
Set all three equal: 4+k = 2k = h → from 4+k=2k get k = 4, then h = 2k = 8. ✔
Discontinuity types & IVT Removable (a hole — limit exists but ≠ f(a)) · Jump (sides differ) · Infinite (vertical asymptote).
Intermediate Value Theorem: if f is continuous on [a,b] and takes opposite signs at the ends, it has a root in between — the standard way to "show an equation has a solution."

Differentiation

The derivative · the rule toolbox · implicit differentiation · log & exp · logarithmic differentiation.

2.1 Meaning

The derivative = slope of the tangent line = instantaneous rate of change

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x+h) − f(x)h

Tangent line at x=a: y = f(a) + f′(a)(x−a).   Differentiable ⟹ continuous (but not the reverse — |x| is continuous yet has a corner at 0, so no derivative there).

2.2 The rule toolbox

The rules you apply on autopilot

Power: d/dx xⁿ = n·xⁿ⁻¹
Constant: d/dx c = 0  ·  Constant×: (c·f)′ = c·f′
Sum: (f ± g)′ = f′ ± g′
Product: (fg)′ = f′g + fg′
Quotient: (f/g)′ = (g·f′ − f·g′)/g²
Chain: d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x))·g′(x)
Trig, exp & log derivatives (know cold) (sin)′=cos · (cos)′=−sin · (tan)′=sec² · (cot)′=−csc² · (sec)′=sec·tan · (csc)′=−csc·cot
(eˣ)′=eˣ · (bˣ)′=bˣ·ln b · (ln x)′=1/x · (log_b x)′=1/(x·ln b). With chain rule, multiply by du/dx.
Where a derivative fails to exist at a discontinuity, a corner (like |x| at 0), or a vertical tangent (like ∛x at 0).
2.3 Implicit differentiation ★

When you can't (or don't want to) solve for y

Two-step recipe ① Differentiate both sides w.r.t. x, treating y as a function of x (so every y gets a ·dy/dx by the chain rule). ② Solve algebraically for dy/dx.
Ex. x² + y² = 2 → differentiate: 2x + 2y·(dy/dx) = 0 → dy/dx = −x/y.
Tangent line at (1,1): slope = −1/1 = −1 → y − 1 = −1(x−1) → y = −x + 2.
Harder. x⁴ + x²y³ − y⁵ = 2x + 1. Differentiate (product rule on x²y³): 4x³ + (2x·y³ + x²·3y²y′) − 5y⁴y′ = 2. Collect y′:
dy/dx = 2 − 4x³ − 2xy³3x²y² − 5y⁴
2.4 Logarithmic differentiation ★

The trick for "variable to a variable power", like xˣ

Three steps ① Take ln of both sides & use log laws to break it up. ② Differentiate implicitly (left side becomes (1/y)·y′). ③ Solve for y′ and replace y with the original f(x).
Ex. y = xˣ. Take ln: ln y = x·ln x.
Differentiate: (1/y)y′ = ln x + x·(1/x) = ln x + 1.
Solve: y′ = y(1 + ln x) → y′ = xˣ(1 + ln x).
Log laws that do the heavy lifting ln(AB)=ln A+ln B · ln(A/B)=ln A−ln B · ln(Aᴮ)=B·ln A. Also great for messy products/quotients like ln( x²√((x−1)³(2x+1)⁷) ) — take ln first, then it's just sums.

L'Hôpital's Rule

Using derivatives to crack indeterminate limits.

3.1 The rule

When you hit 0/0 or ∞/∞, differentiate top and bottom separately

If lim f/g is 0/0 or ∞/∞, then   limx→a f(x)g(x) = limx→a f′(x)g′(x)
Check the form FIRST L'Hôpital is only valid for 0/0 or ∞/∞. Applying it to something like 1/0 or 2/1 gives a wrong answer. You may apply it repeatedly if the form stays indeterminate.
Ex. limx→0 sin x / x = cos x / 1 → cos 0 = 1.
Ex. limx→∞ ln x / eˣ (∞/∞) = (1/x)/eˣ → 0 = 0.
3.2 Other indeterminate forms

Convert them to 0/0 or ∞/∞ first

The disguised forms: ∞−∞, 0·∞, 0⁰, ∞⁰, 1∞. Reshape, then apply L'Hôpital.

0·∞ → make a fraction

Ex. limx→∞ x·sin(1/x) = sin(1/x)/(1/x) (now 0/0) → 1.

1∞, 0⁰, ∞⁰ → take ln, solve, then exponentiate

Recipe Let y = f(x)^{g(x)}. Then ln y = g(x)·ln f(x) (a 0·∞ form). Find L = lim ln y, and the answer is eL.
Ex. limx→∞ (1 − 3/x)2x (form 1∞). ln y = 2x·ln(1−3/x) → −6, so limit = e⁻⁶.
Ex. limx→0 x1/ln x (form 0⁰). ln y = (1/ln x)·ln x = 1, so limit = e.

Linear Approximation & Differentials

Using the tangent line to estimate hard values by hand.

4.1 Linearization

Near x=a, a curve looks like its tangent line

L(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x − a)    and    f(x) ≈ L(x)  (for x near a)
How to pick a Choose an a close to your target where f(a) and f′(a) are easy (a perfect square/cube, or 1).

Ex. Approximate √4.01

f(x)=√x, a=4: f(4)=2, f′(x)=1/(2√x) so f′(4)=¼.
L(x)=2+¼(x−4) → √4.01 ≈ 2+¼(0.01) = 2.0025.  (and √3.95 ≈ 2−¼(0.05)= 1.9875)
Ex. ln(1.1): f=ln x, a=1 → L(x)=0+1·(x−1)=x−1 → ln(1.1)≈ 0.1.
Ex. ∛0.97: f=x^{1/3}, a=1, f′(1)=⅓ → 1+⅓(−0.03)= 0.99.
4.2 Differentials

Estimating error

dy = f′(x)·dx  ≈  Δy  (the small change in f when x changes by dx)
Ex. A cube side is 30 cm ± 0.02. V=x³, dV=3x²·dx = 3(30²)(±0.02) = ±54 cm³. Relative error dV/V = 54/27000 = 1/500.

Set 1 · One-sided & piecewise limits

1. Let f(x) = { (x²+3x+2)/|x+1|, x<−1 ;   2−x², x≥−1 }. Find limx→−1⁻f, limx→−1⁺f, and limx→−1f.

Left (x<−1, so x+1<0 ⟹ |x+1| = −(x+1)): factor x²+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2) → (x+1)(x+2)/[−(x+1)] = −(x+2) → −(−1+2) = −1.

Right (x≥−1): 2−x² → 2−1 = 1.

Left (−1) ≠ Right (1), so limx→−1f does not exist. The absolute value is the trap — it flips the sign on the left side.

2. Evaluate (a) limx→3 (x−3)/(x²−9)   (b) limx→0 (√(x+9)−3)/x.

(a) 0/0. Factor x²−9=(x−3)(x+3) → 1/(x+3) → 1/6 = 1/6.

(b) 0/0. Multiply by conjugate (√(x+9)+3): (x+9−9)/[x(√(x+9)+3)] = 1/(√(x+9)+3) → 1/(3+3) = 1/6.

Set 2 · Trig limits & squeeze theorem

3. Find limx→0 tan(3x)/sin(5x).

Rewrite tan(3x)=sin(3x)/cos(3x) and force the sin(kx)/(kx)→1 pattern:

= [sin(3x)/(3x)]·(3x) / { cos(3x)·[sin(5x)/(5x)]·(5x) } = (1·3x)/(1·1·5x) → 3/5.

4. Show limx→0 x²·sin(1/x) = 0, and use it to decide continuity of g(x)={ x²sin(1/x), x≠0 ; 0, x=0 } at 0.

Since −1 ≤ sin(1/x) ≤ 1, multiply by x²≥0: −x² ≤ x²sin(1/x) ≤ x².

Both limx→0(−x²)=0 and limx→0x²=0, so by the squeeze theorem the middle → 0.

Now check continuity: ① g(0)=0 defined; ② limx→0g=0 exists; ③ they're equal → g is continuous at 0. ✔

Set 3 · Limits at infinity & asymptotes

5. Find all vertical and horizontal asymptotes of f(x) = (3x²+1)/(x²−4).

Vertical: denominator zero where numerator isn't: x²−4=0 → x=±2 → x=2 and x=−2.

Horizontal: equal degrees (2 and 2) → ratio of leading coeffs 3/1 → y=3.

6. Evaluate (a) limx→∞ (√(x²+5x) − x)   (b) limx→−∞ √(x²−8)/(1+2x).

(a) ∞−∞. Conjugate: (x²+5x−x²)/(√(x²+5x)+x) = 5x/(x√(1+5/x)+x) → 5/(1+1) = 5/2.

(b) As x→−∞, √(x²)=|x|=−x. Divide by x: [−x·√(1−8/x²)]/[x(1/x + 2)] → (−1·1)/(0+2) = −1/2. (sign flip because x is negative — the classic trap)

Set 4 · Continuity — find the constants ★

7. Find m so that f(x) = { x/sin(mx), x≠0 ; 3, x=0 } is continuous at 0.

Need limx→0 x/sin(mx) = f(0)=3. Write x/sin(mx) = (1/m)·[mx/sin(mx)] → (1/m)·1 = 1/m.

So 1/m = 3 → m = 1/3.

8. Find a, b so that f(x) = { ax+1, x≤2 ; x²−b, x>2 } is both continuous and differentiable at x=2.

Differentiable ⟹ slopes match: left slope a, right slope 2x|₂=4 → a = 4.

Continuous ⟹ pieces meet: a(2)+1 = 2²−b → 8+1 = 4−b → b = 4−9 = −5.

Check: left 4x+1 and right x²+5 both give 9 at x=2, slopes both 4. ✔

Set 5 · Product / quotient / chain

9. Differentiate (a) y=(2+√x+x)(x³−3x+1)   (b) F(x)=cos³(sin x)   (c) y=sin¹⁰(2x+1).

(a) Product rule (fg)′=f′g+fg′ with f′ = 1/(2√x)+1: y′ = (1/(2√x)+1)(x³−3x+1) + (2+√x+x)(3x²−3).

(b) Chain, 3 layers (outer cube · cos · inner sin): F′ = 3cos²(sin x)·(−sin(sin x))·cos x.

(c) y=[sin(2x+1)]¹⁰ → y′ = 10 sin⁹(2x+1)·cos(2x+1)·2.

10. Differentiate y = (2 + x√x)/(x³+1).

Quotient rule (f/g)′=(g f′ − f g′)/g², with f = 2 + x^{3/2}, f′ = (3/2)x^{1/2}, g = x³+1, g′=3x²:

y′ = [ (x³+1)(3/2)√x − (2+x√x)(3x²) ] / (x³+1)².

Set 6 · Implicit differentiation ★

11. For x³ + y³ = 6xy (the folium), find dy/dx.

Differentiate (product rule on 6xy): 3x² + 3y²y′ = 6y + 6xy′.

Collect y′: y′(3y² − 6x) = 6y − 3x² → dy/dx = (6y−3x²)/(3y²−6x) = (2y−x²)/(y²−2x).

12. For y² = x³ + 3x²: find the slope at (1, 2) and the tangent line.

2y·y′ = 3x² + 6x → y′ = (3x²+6x)/(2y). At (1,2): (3+6)/(4) = 9/4.

Tangent: y − 2 = (9/4)(x − 1) → y = (9/4)x − 1/4.

13. For x² + y² = 2, find the second derivative d²y/dx².

First: 2x+2yy′=0 → y′=−x/y. Differentiate y′=−x/y with the quotient rule (remember y′=−x/y):

y″ = −[ y·1 − x·y′ ]/y² = −[ y − x(−x/y) ]/y² = −(y²+x²)/y³. Since x²+y²=2: y″ = −2/y³.

Set 7 · Logarithmic differentiation ★

14. Differentiate y = x^{\,sin x}.

Take ln: ln y = sin x · ln x. Differentiate (product rule on the right):

(1/y)y′ = cos x·ln x + sin x·(1/x) → y′ = x^{sin x}( cos x·ln x + sin x / x ).

15. With a,b,c,d constants, differentiate f(x) = (x−2)ᵃ(x−3)ᵇ / [ (x+4)ᶜ(x+5)ᵈ ].

Take ln and split with log laws: ln f = a·ln(x−2)+b·ln(x−3)−c·ln(x+4)−d·ln(x+5).

Differentiate: f′/f = a/(x−2)+b/(x−3)−c/(x+4)−d/(x+5), so

f′ = f·[ a/(x−2)+b/(x−3)−c/(x+4)−d/(x+5) ] (a nightmare with the quotient rule, trivial with logs).

Set 8 · L'Hôpital — every form

16. limx→0 (eˣ − 1 − x)/x²

0/0 → (eˣ−1)/(2x) still 0/0 → eˣ/2 → 1/2 = 1/2 (applied twice).

17. limx→0⁺ x·ln x  (form 0·∞)

Rewrite as a fraction: ln x/(1/x) (form −∞/∞) → L'Hôpital (1/x)/(−1/x²) = −x → 0 = 0.

18. limx→∞ (1 + 3/x)ˣ  (form 1∞)

Let y=(1+3/x)ˣ, so ln y = x·ln(1+3/x). Sub t=1/x→0⁺: ln(1+3t)/t (0/0) → 3/(1+3t) → 3.

So lim ln y = 3 ⟹ answer e³.

19. limx→0 ( 1/x − 1/sin x )  (form ∞−∞)

Common denominator: (sin x − x)/(x sin x) (0/0). L'Hôpital: (cos x − 1)/(sin x + x cos x) (still 0/0).

Again: (−sin x)/(2cos x − x sin x) → 0/2 = 0.

Set 9 · Linear approximation & error

20. Use linearization to approximate (a) √50   (b) (8.06)^{1/3}.

(a) f=√x, a=49: f(49)=7, f′(49)=1/(2·7)=1/14. √50 ≈ 7 + (1/14)(1) ≈ 7.0714.

(b) f=x^{1/3}, a=8: f(8)=2, f′(8)=(1/3)·8^{−2/3}=1/12. (8.06)^{1/3} ≈ 2 + (1/12)(0.06) = 2.005.

21. A circle's radius is measured as 10 cm ± 0.1. Use differentials to estimate the maximum error in its area.

A=πr², dA = 2πr·dr = 2π(10)(0.1) = 2π ≈ 6.28 cm². Relative error dA/A = 2πr dr/(πr²) = 2dr/r = 0.02 = 2%.

Set 10 · Abstract chain rule (the signature hard one) ★

22. Let h(x) = f(sin(2x) + f(2x)) where f(0)=0 and f′(0)=2. Find h′(0) and the tangent line to y=h(x) at x=0.

Chain rule — outer f′ at the inside, times the derivative of the inside:

h′(x) = f′( sin 2x + f(2x) ) · ( 2cos 2x + 2f′(2x) ).

At x=0: inside = sin 0 + f(0) = 0, so the outer factor is f′(0)=2. Bracket = 2cos0 + 2f′(0) = 2 + 4 = 6.

h′(0) = 2·6 = 12. And h(0)=f(sin0+f(0))=f(0)=0, so the tangent line is y = 12x.

On the real exam they push this further to h″(0) — same idea, differentiate h′ again with the product + chain rule and plug in the given f″(0).

Toolkit Same base ⟹ equate exponents. Hidden quadratic ⟹ let u=eˣ (or 5ˣ). Log rules: ln A + ln B = ln(AB), ln A − ln B = ln(A/B). Continuous growth: A=A₀e^{kt}; rate A′=kA; doubling time t=ln2/k.

Factor out 2^{x−1}: 2^{x−1}(4 + 2 + 1) = 56 → 7·2^{x−1} = 56 → 2^{x−1} = 8 = 2³.

So x−1 = 3 → x = 4.

So eˣ=3 or eˣ=4 → x = ln 3 or x = ln 4.

Double: e^{0.05t}=2 → 0.05t = ln 2 → t = ln2/0.05 ≈ 13.86 years.

Quick concept checks. Tap to reveal correct/incorrect + the reason.

Score: 0 / 0

1. limx→0 sin(x)/x = ?

One of the two must-know trig limits: sin(x)/x → 1 as x→0. (The other: (1−cos x)/x → 0.)

Left limit = right limit (both finite). f(a) itself is irrelevant to whether the limit exists.

All three: defined, limit exists, and they're equal. (Differentiable ⟹ continuous, but continuous does NOT ⟹ differentiable — think |x| at 0.)

Variable base and exponent → logarithmic differentiation: ln y = x ln x → y′/y = ln x + 1 → y′ = xˣ(1+ln x).

Two sentences to write + 6 multiple-choice. Only two tenses are tested.

Present Continuous (pp.10–12)

am / is / are + verb-ing

Use it for: actions happening now or around now, temporary situations, and current trends (very common in economics).

Signal words now, right now, at the moment, currently, these days, today.
  • "The Prime Minister is sleeping right now."
  • "Prices are rising at the moment."
  • "Unemployment is falling this year."
Watch out Stative verbs (know, want, believe, own) normally aren't used in the continuous.
Present Perfect (pp.31–32)

have / has + past participle

Use it for: a past action with a present result/relevance, or something that started in the past and continues now (with since / for).

Signal words since, for, already, yet, ever, never, just, recently, so far.
  • "The war has caused prices to rise since March."
  • "GDP has grown for three years."
  • "The bank has already cut rates."
since vs for since + a point in time (since March, since 2020). for + a duration (for 3 years, for a decade).
Exam tip — how to choose Ask: is it happening right now / a current trend? → Present Continuous. Did it start in the past and still matter now (esp. with since/for)? → Present Perfect. The prompt words (now vs since) are your biggest clue.

Academic Writing — Formal Style (5 pts, pp.33–38)

You'll rewrite an informal paragraph in formal academic English. Apply these rules.

The rewrite rules
  1. No contractions: don't → do not, it's → it is, can't → cannot.
  2. Replace phrasal verbs with single formal verbs: find out → discover, go up → increase, put off → postpone, cut down → reduce, look into → investigate.
  3. No slang / vague words: "a lot of" → a significant amount / many, "kids" → children, "stuff" → items, "get" → obtain / receive.
  4. Impersonal & objective: avoid "I think", "you"; use hedging (may, appears to, suggests) and often the passive.
  5. No emotional language / exclamation marks / rhetorical questions.
Informal ✗"Prices have gone up a lot and it's really bad. I think the government should do something about it ASAP!"
Formal ✓"Prices have increased significantly, which appears to have had a negative effect. It may be advisable for the government to intervene promptly."

Private · Influence & Persuasion

The psychology of influence — how four classic techniques actually work

Four well-studied mechanisms — the Franklin effect, the funnel technique, the IKEA effect, and Ericksonian (indirect) suggestion — sit behind a huge amount of everyday sales, marketing, management and negotiation. Each entry below gives you the mechanism, the research it rests on, how it shows up in social and corporate settings, how the four link together, and — most important for actually understanding it — how to recognise and resist it.

Influence vs. manipulation — the line Persuasion becomes manipulation when it is covert (you'd object if you saw the technique), exploitative (benefits one side by using your biases against you), and irreversible (engineered so you can't easily back out). The single best test: would this survive disclosure? If the other person would feel used the moment they understood what you did, it was manipulation. Learn these to defend yourself first.
Honesty noteThese effects are real and replicated — but the internet oversells them. "Covert hypnosis can control anyone against their will" is not supported by evidence, and most strong NLP claims have failed empirical tests. Where a claim is shaky, this page says so.
How each entry is graded Every technique below is a catalogue entry carrying an evidence grade: A · replicated experiment B · single / contested study C · clinical or field lore …plus the key source, the strongest objection, a verbatim script, failure tells, a 10-minute drill, and what it stacks with. Empirical claims are tagged Certain Likely Guessing.
1

The Ben Franklin Effect

"He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than he whom you yourself have obliged."

The effect: when someone does you a small favour, they tend to like you more afterwards — the opposite of the intuition that we help people we already like. Franklin, in his autobiography, won over a hostile rival legislator by asking to borrow a rare book. Having done the favour, the rival became friendly for life.

Mechanism — cognitive dissonance / self-perception The helper's mind holds two facts: "I went out of my way for this person" and "I don't especially like them." Those clash. The cheapest way to resolve the clash is to adjust the attitude: "I helped them → I must actually like/respect them." We infer our feelings from our own behaviour.
EvidenceJecker & Landy (1969): participants who were personally asked by the researcher to return a cash prize (a favour to him) later rated him more likeable than those who weren't asked.

In the wild — social & corporate

  • "Ask for advice, not approval." Asking a senior colleague for a small piece of help or mentorship builds their investment in you.
  • Sales/onboarding: get the prospect to do a tiny thing for you (send a doc, pick a slot) — small giving breeds warmth.
  • Team-building: reciprocal small favours knit groups together faster than gifts do.
How it links →It's the mirror of reciprocity (there, they give to you). A favour is also the first micro-commitment, which feeds straight into the Funnel (§2) and the effort-investment of the IKEA effect (§3).
🛡 DefenseWhen a small favour is followed by a bigger ask, separate the two thoughts: "I helped them" does not mean "I trust their proposal." Judge the ask on its merits.

The Funnel Technique

Start broad and easy; narrow to the commitment. Every small "yes" makes the next one cheaper.

Two related meanings share the shape of a funnel:

  1. Funnel questioning (interviews, sales discovery, UX research): begin with open, broad questions to build rapport and gather context, then move to closed, specific ones that pin down a decision.
  2. Commitment funnel (foot-in-the-door): secure a trivial agreement first, then escalate. Each agreement lowers resistance to the next.
Mechanism — consistency & momentum Once you've said yes, saying no next feels like contradicting yourself. Cialdini's commitment & consistency principle: people strive to act consistently with what they've already said and done. A "yes-set" builds behavioural momentum.
Evidence & relativesFreedman & Fraser (1966), foot-in-the-door: agreeing to a tiny request (a small sign) tripled compliance with a large one later. Cousins: low-ball, and the marketing AIDA funnel (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action).

In the wild — social & corporate

  • Sales scripts (e.g. SPIN): situation → problem → implication → need-payoff, each step a smaller gap to the close.
  • Growth/marketing funnels: free content → email → trial → paid, each a low-friction step.
  • Negotiation: agree on principles first ("we both want a fair deal?") before numbers.
How it links →Each early "yes" is a Franklin-style micro-commitment (§1). The "yes-set" is exactly the pacing move in Ericksonian language (§4). And asking the person to do a step invokes the IKEA effect (§3).
🛡 DefenseNotice a run of trivial agreements. Only the last decision has real stakes — evaluate it on its own, as if the earlier yeses never happened. "I agreed to those, but this one I'll decide fresh."
Catalogue entryGrade Asales-script version: B
1Definition

A sequenced escalation from a trivial agreement to the target request, exploiting the drive to stay consistent with a commitment you've already made.

2Evidence — Grade A (experimental core)

Freedman & Fraser (1966) foot-in-the-door Certain; meta-analysed by Burger (1999), which confirms a small but reliable effect Likely, moderated by delay, requester similarity, and the absence of an external incentive. Strongest objection: effect sizes are modest and vanish when the first ask is too trivial to shift self-image, or when a reward explains the first "yes." The "funnel questioning" sales script is B/C lore, not the same thing as the experiment. Rebranding of: commitment & consistency — FITD, the AIDA funnel, and SPIN selling are three coats of paint on it.

3Mechanism

Self-perception ("I'm the kind of person who says yes to this") + agreement momentum. Parent principle: consistency.

4Script

Two easy trues, then a small concrete ask carrying its own bind (Thu/Fri). Never open with the big request.

5Failure modes

a) First ask isn't trivial — a heavy opener triggers reactance and kills the ladder. Tell: they push back on step one ("what's this actually about?").
b) Incentivised first "yes" — if you paid for or pressured the first agreement, no consistency forms. Tell: they treat every step as a fresh negotiation; no momentum carries.

6Drill — 10 min, pass/fail

The four-yes ladder. Write four true, small pacing statements in advance, deliver them naturally, then one real lead. Pass = four agreements (nod / "yeah/right/mm") AND movement on the lead. Fail = any pacer draws a correction, or no action on the lead.

7Adjacent

Stacks with: pacing (a verbal yes-set), the IKEA effect (a step that costs effort). Contradicts: the one-shot "take it or leave it" close, and door-in-the-face, which runs the opposite direction — a large ask first, then concede down.

8Content hook

The IKEA Effect

Labour leads to love: we over-value what we partly built ourselves.

The effect: people assign disproportionately high value to objects they partially created — assembling the IKEA cabinet, folding the origami, building the Lego — compared with identical objects they didn't build.

Mechanism — effort justification + ownership Two forces stack: effort justification ("I worked on it, so it must be worth it") and a heightened sense of ownership/competence. It's the endowment effect supercharged by self-investment. Boundary condition: the boost only appears when the build succeeds — abandon it half-finished and the effect vanishes.
EvidenceNorton, Mochon & Ariely (2012): people paid 63% more for self-assembled IKEA boxes than for pre-assembled identical ones, and valued their own origami nearly as highly as an expert's.

In the wild — social & corporate

  • Co-creation & configurators: "build your own" cars, PCs, meal kits — customer effort raises attachment and willingness to pay.
  • Management buy-in: "people support what they help build" — invite staff to shape a plan and they defend it as theirs.
  • Onboarding: make users set up a profile / customise the app early → higher retention.
How it links →The Funnel (§2) is what gets you to invest that first bit of effort; the IKEA effect then makes you over-value the result; consistency locks you into defending it. A completed build is also a big Franklin-style self-investment (§1).
🛡 DefenseSeparate the value of your labour from the market value of the thing. Ask: "If a stranger built this, what would I pay?" Close cousin of the sunk-cost trap.

Ericksonian (Indirect) Suggestion

Milton Erickson's permissive, conversational style — match the person's reality first, then lead.

Milton H. Erickson (1901–1980) was a psychiatrist who rejected the authoritarian "you are getting sleepy" style for an indirect, permissive one that uses the person's own language and experience. Its therapeutic aim was to bypass a client's conscious resistance to help them change. The same devices appear — often crudely — in sales, copywriting and negotiation.

Core devices (the "Milton model")
  • Pacing & leading: describe what's undeniably true right now (pacing) to build a "yes" rhythm, then append the suggestion (leading).
  • Presupposition: smuggle a claim in as a given — "When you start noticing improvements…" (assumes you will).
  • Double bind: offer two options that both lead where the speaker wants — "Would you like to begin now, or after a coffee?"
  • Embedded command & artful vagueness: a directive nested in a sentence; deliberately unspecific language the listener fills with their own meaning.
Honesty note — what's real vs. hypeClinical hypnosis is a genuine, studied phenomenon (e.g. for pain and anxiety). But "covert conversational hypnosis that controls people without consent" is not established science, and NLP's specific claims have largely failed controlled testing. Treat pacing/leading as rapport and framing, not mind control.

In the wild — social & corporate

  • Rapport-building: matching tone, pace and language before making a proposal.
  • Copywriting: presuppositions ("when you upgrade…") and embedded commands.
  • Negotiation: the double bind as a false choice that hides the "no" option.
How it links →Pacing is the Funnel's yes-set (§2) in language form. The double bind is a two-band funnel with the exit removed. Rapport is Franklin/reciprocity warmth (§1). Together they're the delivery layer of the stack below.

  6. Slow the tempo. "I'll decide by tomorrow." Manipulation runs on momentum and urgency.
  7. Apply the disclosure test. Would I feel used if I saw the technique? If yes, walk.
Use it ethicallyTo persuade without manipulating: be willing to show your reasoning, make the "no" genuinely available, and make sure the other side also benefits. Influence you'd be comfortable explaining afterwards is influence you can be proud of.
